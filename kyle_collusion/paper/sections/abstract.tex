\begin{abstract}
Q-learning informed traders in Kyle markets trade far less than competition
implies, earning supracompetitive profits and making prices less informative.
Whether this is \emph{collusion}, sustained by the threat of punishment, or a
\emph{learning bias} that prunes aggressive orders matters for policy: the
first calls for limits on transparency, the second for standards on algorithm
design. We separate the two with a paired rival-deviation test and three
placebos that cannot collude: myopic learners, an uninformative memory, and
passive traders that move the cartel above Nash. Analytically, a one-period
deviation in the standard Kyle market moves order flow by only $(I-1)/(2I)$
noise standard deviations per unit of the value signal, whatever the noise
volume; with many information-insensitive investors the ratio is in the
hundreds. In simulations, Q-learners close \ResidDeltaInt{} of the gap from
competition to the cartel, yet never punish deviations, even with perfect
monitoring, and deviating pays. Myopic learners trade even less
(\MyopicResidDeltaInt{}), an uninformative memory reproduces the outcome, and
with passive traders learners trade half their Nash intensity although
collusion would mean trading more. A lone trader with no rival under-trades by
up to 57\%, in proportion to the square root of the step size. Reducing
transparency changes nothing (\OpaqueDeltaInt{}); counterfactual updating
restores competition (\CfResidDeltaInt{}). \DouAbstractSentence{} We release a
validated, CPU-only testbed with exact benchmarks, tabular and deep RL agents,
and all diagnostics.
\end{abstract}
