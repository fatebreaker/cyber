To check why a myopic shared-table learner reacts to large noise shocks at
$\xi=500$, we trained one more time (20 markets, $1.5\times10^7$ periods,
otherwise as in Table~\ref{tab:shared}), then recorded which price states greedy
play visits and the greedy trading intensity in every state, expressed on a
scale where 0 is the cartel and 1 is Nash.

Greedy play visits only the two outermost price-surprise bins
(together 100.0\% of visits). Because the learners trade well below
Nash, the realised price is hundreds of noise standard deviations away from the
Nash-expected price that our state is centred on, so the surprise saturates
the outer bins. In those visited states the learned intensity is close to the
cartel (0.29--0.29 on the 0--1 scale). The thirteen interior
bins are never visited in greedy play, and their greedy orders remain close to
the initialisation, which favours Nash-like trading (0.79--0.81).

This explains the shock response. A shock in the direction of $v$ moves the
price towards the Nash-expected price and hence the next state towards the
interior bins. Small shocks do not reach them; large ones do, and there the
learner trades as it was initialised, harder than on path. The resulting
threshold-shaped reaction is not a learned punishment but the behaviour of a
learner in states it never learned about.

Two caveats follow. First, our price state bins the surprise in units of
noise-driven price variation, which saturates at $\xi=500$; \citet{dou2025}
bin the price over the range implied by the order grid, which may leave more
states on path. Our replication therefore does not reproduce their state
space exactly, and the separate-table learners in Section~\ref{sec:dou} saw
an effectively coarse price signal. Second, the general lesson does not depend
on the binning: whenever shocks move a Q-learner into rarely or never visited
states, its reaction reflects initialisation and incomplete exploration rather
than strategy, which is exactly what the myopic placebo detects.
