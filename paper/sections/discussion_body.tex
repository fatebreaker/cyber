\paragraph{What the diagnostics show.} The supracompetitive outcomes of
Q-learning informed traders do not rest on punishment: rivals do not react to
deviations, deviating pays, placebos that cannot collude reproduce both the
outcome and the reactions that have been read as punishment, and the outcome
moves with the learning rule rather than with what traders can observe. The
same tests find punishment in the pricing game where it is known to exist.

\paragraph{Implications for auditing algorithms.} Section~\ref{sec:audit}
turns the diagnostics into a protocol. Its value is that every step can fail:
applied to the \citet{calvano2020} baseline it finds punishment, and applied
to informed traders it does not.

\paragraph{Implications for policy.} If supracompetitive trading is a learning
bias, interventions aimed at collusion through information, such as delayed or
coarser order-flow disclosure, will not work; our reduced-transparency
treatment confirms this. What works is algorithm design: counterfactual
(full-information) updating removes the bias. This mirrors conclusions of
\citet{asker2022,asker2024} and \citet{abada2023} for pricing algorithms and
extends them to informed trading. A bias is not harmless: it still lowers
liquidity and price informativeness, myopic learners push trading below even
the cartel level, and with passive traders it costs the learners themselves
\PassiveProfitPct\% of their equilibrium profit. But its remedy is a
design standard, not a cartel investigation.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our evidence concerns tabular Q-learning and, more
briefly, DQN and PPO; learners that model their rivals may find punishment
strategies these do not. Constant-step Q-learning does not converge in the
strict sense of \citet{calvano2020}, although outcomes are stable across seeds,
schedules and horizons up to $2\times10^9$ periods. Our replication of
\citet{dou2025} matches every element of their published protocol, but not
their code; we cannot rule out that other implementation details let their
learners discover punishment, which is why the placebo, not the absence of a
response, carries our argument there. Finally, the stylised market abstracts
from inventory dynamics, multiple assets and long-lived information.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Low trading by learning algorithms is easy to produce
and easy to misread; telling collusion from learning bias requires
diagnostics that can fail. Among informed traders, even where deviations are
most visible, they point to pruning, not punishment.
